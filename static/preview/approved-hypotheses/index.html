BRS5-FM2-PM4 · PM4-H1

Hypothesis-1 — Plant-Substrate Variety and Microbial Processing Capacity Unestablished

Scientific hypothesis. Scientific admission is required before any proposed mechanism relationship can be approved, including when a hypothesis is marked Supported.

Proposition: At matched total fermentable-substrate intake, a defined increase in plant-substrate variety increases the breadth of microbial processing of a prespecified glycan panel in the tested population. Changes in functional community composition, including but not necessarily greater microbial richness, may mediate this response.

Proposed direction: Plant-substrate variety → microbial substrate-processing breadth

Rationale: Defined-community experiments support substrate- and organism-specific processing, while human dietary studies motivate testing plant-substrate variety. Community richness is a candidate mediator, not a validated surrogate for processing capacity.

Source evidence and its role
  • Patnode et al. (2019) (contextual) — Defined community membership, competition and glycan exposure influence processing. Gnotobiotic mouse experiments; not a general causal plant-variety or richness benefit in humans.
  • McDonald et al. (2018) (contextual) — Plant-variety categories were associated with microbiome measures. Observational self-reported diet; no causal processing-flux or optimal threshold result.
  • Wastyk et al. (2021) (contextual) — Human fibre and fermented-food interventions separate functional potential, diversity and immune outcomes. Gene abundance is not utilisation flux; no demonstrated richness mediation.
  • Stanford et al. (2025) (contextual) — A CKD crossover tested higher versus lower plant variety. Population-specific and composition-confounded; no consistent overall uremic-toxin reduction.
  • Creedon et al. (2026) (contextual) — A mixed-plant product changed composition and some symptoms, without significant between-group microbiome health-rank differences. Product-specific; no demonstrated processing breadth or plant-count effect. Full-text richness detail was not independently reverified.

References to the same study or cohort are not independent evidence. Finding scores are not inherited.

Principal limitations
  • Plant count does not specify substrate chemistry, portions or preparation; matching fibre grams does not match chemistry.
  • CAZyme abundance, stool metabolite concentrations and microbial richness do not measure utilisation flux.
  • Greater richness need not improve processing; functional replacement, redundancy and competition may alter outcomes.
  • No universal 30-plants threshold, barrier effect, cognitive benefit or clinical efficacy is established.
  • Studies of different diets and populations do not establish the complete causal chain.
Alternative or interacting explanations
  • Specific substrate chemistry or degrader abundance rather than richness may explain processing.
  • Transit, microbial biomass, adherence and food preparation may explain apparent differences.
Evidence required
  • Compare characterised dietary patterns at matched fermentable-substrate intake and energy, measuring a prespecified glycan-utilisation panel.
  • Measure community composition, enzyme activity and actual processing separately; distinguish ex-vivo capacity from in-vivo flux.
  • A precise processing null despite verified exposure counts against the intervention proposition; processing gains without richness gains count against richness mediation, not necessarily the intervention.

Hypothesis-1 — Additional Arginine Supply and Cerebral Creatine Availability Unestablished

Scientific hypothesis. Scientific admission is required before any proposed mechanism relationship can be approved, including when a hypothesis is marked Supported.

Proposition: In an adult experimental model with adequate background glycine and methionine supply, additional oral arginine increases its contribution to cerebral creatine synthesis or delivery and increases cerebral creatine availability compared with an isonitrogenous control.

Proposed direction: Additional oral arginine → cerebral creatine contribution and availability

Rationale: Renal precursor-production experiments motivate testing cerebral availability. Existing arginine, glycine and methionine synthesis-capacity admissions do not establish benefit from additional intake.

Supporting Findings

None identified as directly testing this proposition.

Contradictory or null Findings

None identified as directly testing this proposition.

Contextual Findings

  • BRS4-FM1-PM3 / PM3-F7 — Provides bounded pathway context; does not establish the complete hypothesis.
Source evidence and its role
  • Edison et al. (2007) (contextual) — Renal GAA production and acute rat arginine/citrulline responses provide precursor context; glycine infusion was null. Not an oral human brain experiment.
  • Asiriwardhana et al. (2025) (contextual) — Dietary methionine influences methyl allocation to products including creatine in neonatal piglets. Hepatic neonatal context, not adult cerebral delivery.
  • Solis et al. (2014) (contextual) — Vegetarians and omnivores had similar measured brain total creatine despite intake differences. Cross-sectional; not a direct precursor intervention or refutation of this hypothesis.

References to the same study or cohort are not independent evidence. Finding scores are not inherited.

Principal limitations
  • Renal GAA production and neonatal hepatic methyl incorporation are not cerebral delivery measurements.
  • Arginine infusion is not oral exposure; glycine infusion did not produce the same response.
  • AGAT feedback, methyl allocation and tissue transport constrain supply.
  • Brain isotope appearance does not distinguish local synthesis from peripheral synthesis and import; greater labelling need not increase the total pool.
  • No cognitive benefit, effective food dose or new dietary admission is established.
  • The full 2025 piglet tracer protocol was not independently verified in this appraisal.
Alternative or interacting explanations
  • Peripheral synthesis may rise without greater brain uptake.
  • Turnover or replacement may change without increased pool availability; transport and baseline sufficiency may govern response.
Evidence required
  • Measure oral exposure, peripheral GAA/creatine production, region-specific brain incorporation and total creatine/PCr with precursor-enrichment correction.
  • Support requires increased cerebral contribution and availability; greater labelling alone supports turnover only.
  • A precise cerebral null despite verified exposure and a peripheral response counts against the bounded proposition.

Hypothesis-1 — Dietary Phosphatidylethanolamine and Neural NAPE/NAE Precursor Supply Unestablished

Scientific hypothesis. Scientific admission is required before any proposed mechanism relationship can be approved, including when a hypothesis is marked Supported.

Proposition: Dietary phosphatidylethanolamine may contribute to neural NAPE precursor availability and subsequent N-acylethanolamine formation. Whether this contribution is quantitatively meaningful under ordinary dietary exposure remains unresolved.

Proposed direction: Dietary phosphatidylethanolamine → neural NAPE precursor availability → N-acylethanolamine formation

Rationale: Intracellular NAPE precursor biology provides a rationale for examining dietary contribution. Oral delivery to the relevant neural pools and its quantitative importance are not established.

Supporting Findings

Contradictory or null Findings

Contextual Findings

Source evidence and its role

  • Lithander et al. (2008) (contextual) — Oral PE-containing exposure did not improve satiety relative to lipid control. Neural precursor supply was not measured; the behavioural null does not resolve incorporation.
  • Oatmeal trial registration NCT03468179 (contextual) — The saved citation describes a proposed oatmeal NAPE/NAE experiment. Not verified results, not oat-bran equivalence or a PE-specific neural delivery result.

References to the same study or cohort are not independent evidence. Finding scores are not inherited.

Principal limitations
  • Digestion and remodelling may separate dietary atoms from the intact PE molecule.
  • Trace incorporation alone does not establish meaningful precursor supply, increased pool availability or altered synthesis.
  • PE, NAPE and NAE molecular species must remain distinct; evidence for one does not generalise to all NAEs or anandamide.
  • Gut or plasma measurements do not establish neural supply.
  • No increased endocannabinoid signalling, clinical benefit or ordinary-food equivalence is established.
  • The saved oatmeal trial citation describes a proposal, not verified doubled plasma levels; preserve the source-page discrepancy for separate correction.
  • No animal model or arbitrarily selected PE species is part of the core proposition; no experiment is authorised by registration.
Alternative or interacting explanations
  • Endogenous synthesis may dominate neural pools.
  • Dietary-derived atoms may be recycled without a meaningful contribution to precursor availability.
Evidence required
  • Characterise dietary exposure, digestion, absorption and remodelling and establish contribution to relevant neural NAPE and specified NAE products.
  • Quantify contribution relative to endogenous synthesis and pool turnover; distinguish detectable trace contribution from meaningful supply.
  • Isotope tracing, lipid measurements and controlled exposures are possible methods, not authorised studies.
  • Negligible contribution despite verified exposure counts against meaningful supply under those conditions without rejecting established PE biology.

BRS-X(Hormones-PM6) · PM6-H1

Hypothesis-1 — Microbial Androgen Deconjugation and Systemic Re-exposure Unestablished

Scientific hypothesis. Scientific admission is required before any proposed mechanism relationship can be approved, including when a hypothesis is marked Supported.

Proposition: Intestinal microbial deconjugation of host-derived androgen glucuronides may contribute to systemic re-exposure to unconjugated androgens. The magnitude of this contribution and its influence on circulating concentrations and tissue exposure remain unresolved.

Proposed direction: Microbial androgen deconjugation → systemic androgen re-exposure

Rationale: Experimental androgen-receptor studies provide a rationale for investigating whether microbiome-dependent androgen exposure could influence stress-response regulation. They do not establish that intestinal androgen processing changes neural receptor engagement, HPA responses or allostatic adaptation.

Supporting Findings

None identified as directly testing this proposition.

Contradictory or null Findings

None identified as directly testing this proposition.

Contextual Findings
  • BRS-X(Hormones-PM6) / PM6-F1 — Provides bounded pathway context; does not establish the complete hypothesis.
Source evidence and its role
  • Colldén et al. (2019) (contextual) — Microbiota affect intestinal androgen deconjugation; serum testosterone/DHT did not differ and some tissue androgens were higher in germ-free mice. No traced systemic re-entry or stress endpoint; germ-free and human stool/serum comparisons are distinct.
  • Chen et al. (2014) (contextual) — Testosterone/AR conditions affected anxiety-related measures and corticosterone profiles in mice. Constitutive AR loss; acute endocrine time course, not microbial exposure or allostatic adaptation. No significant group-by-time interaction; capsule-length reporting inconsistent.
  • Chen et al. (2021) (contextual) — Abstract reports region-specific cFos differences under testosterone treatment and AR disruption. Full methods unverified; marker differences do not establish regional causal mediation or microbial hormone delivery.

References to the same study or cohort are not independent evidence. Finding scores are not inherited.

Principal limitations
  • The full gut → androgen → stress → allostasis chain remains explicitly Unestablished.
  • High faecal androgen concentrations do not demonstrate absorption or systemic flux; host synthesis, clearance and feedback are alternatives.
  • Colldén reports serum testosterone/DHT nulls and higher androgen levels in some germ-free tissues, opposing a simple positive systemic-supply model.
  • Testosterone, DHT and their conjugates are distinct; neither a net circulating increase nor benefit is assumed.
  • Constitutive AR disruption confounds developmental effects with adult hormone action. The 2014 group-by-time interaction was not significant; apparent recovery differences require qualification.
  • The 2021 receptor paper full methods were inaccessible; its abstract is contextual only, not fully appraised regional mechanistic support. cFos is not proof of causal regional mediation.
  • Anxiety-like behaviour, HPA activation, acute recovery and repeated-demand allostatic adaptation are different outcomes.
  • BRS5 is process context; the owner remains BRS-X(Hormones-PM6). BRS6-FM2 relevance is contextual, not an accepted PM edge. Allostasis is network-level.
  • No dietary modulation, food connection, neural exposure or complete stress pathway is established.
Alternative or interacting explanations
  • Host endocrine compensation, synthesis and clearance can explain tissue differences.
  • Germ-free developmental effects and non-androgen microbial signals may affect stress independently of re-entry.
Evidence required
  • Establish microbially attributable absorption, hormone identity, exposure magnitude and disposition separately from host synthesis.
  • Linked downstream question: If microbial androgen deconjugation contributes meaningfully to host exposure, does that contribution alter androgen-receptor-dependent stress responses or recovery?
  • Resolve neural target engagement and receptor-dependent effects separately; repeated-demand recovery and physiological costs are required for an allostatic claim.
  • Tracer and enzyme-specific perturbations are possible methods, not an authorised experiment; preserve systemic-concentration nulls alongside any flux measurements.
