## Overview

Neuroeshot is a one- or two-mouthful appetiser that makes a small serving of salmon roe feel distinctive rather than dutiful. Chilled roe pearls bring salinity, umami and a delicate pop; bright lemon acidity follows, with a brief fermented-chilli finish.

This recipe uses a small portion of cured salmon roe. The nutrition panel uses salted chum-salmon ikura. No sodium total is published, because the hot sauce varies by brand.

## Ingredients (1 serving)

- 15 g (1 tbsp) **salmon roe**
- 7.5 ml (1 1/2 tsp) **lemon juice**
- 2 ml **fermented hot sauce** (Tabasco-style)

<RecipeCalculationDefault details={frontMatter} />

## Method

1. Add roe to a small shot glass or spoon.
2. Top with lemon juice.
3. Add 2 ml of fermented hot sauce.
4. Serve immediately.

**Serving interpretation — this recipe only.** “Meaningful” and “minor” describe nutritional contribution, not PM efficacy or a score. Vitamin comparisons use the site’s adult reference basis: [B12 2.4 µg/day](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#nih_ods_b12_reference), [folate 400 µg dietary folate equivalents/day](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#nih_ods_folate_reference) and [vitamin C 90 mg/day](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#nih_ods_vitc_reference) (adult male value, following the site’s higher-sex-value convention; women’s RDA is 75 mg). Naturally occurring food folate converts 1:1 to dietary folate equivalents. These comparisons concern non-pregnant, non-lactating adults and intake, not the amount absorbed. The roe provides **540 mg EPA+DHA combined**, compared with [EFSA’s adult adequate intake of 250 mg/day](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#efsa_fats_drv_2010); this is one combined benchmark, not separate EPA and DHA requirements or a target for brain effects. No universal percentage cutoff is applied. Capsaicin’s sensory action is supported experimentally, but this sauce serving has not been tested for perceived intensity or systemic effects. Unknown quantities remain unestablished, not zero.

Food composition establishes constituent supply, while mechanistic studies explain possible biological roles. Neither alone demonstrates that this completed recipe changes a pathway or produces a clinical benefit.

## Extra Guidance

- The calculation applies to salted chum-salmon roe; another roe preparation must not inherit these nutrient quantities.
- Serve as a one- or two-mouthful appetiser, not as a meal or a supplement-style dose.
- Keep the roe chilled and mix immediately before serving so the pearls retain their texture against the lemon and chilli.
- This composition record does not report total choline. Phosphatidylcholine is reported separately as present in salmon roe.
- Fermented hot sauce is salted, and the salt varies by brand, so the recipe does not publish a sodium total. Roe sodium is the salted ikura figure on the salmon roe page.
- Fermented hot sauce contributes flavour at this quantity. Because products vary and may be pasteurised, this small serving is a flavouring, not a probiotic intervention.

## Brain Health Notes

- **Roe is the nutritional centre of the recipe.** It combines preformed EPA and DHA, documented phosphatidylcholine occurrence, vitamin B12, astaxanthin and high-quality protein at unusually high density for such a small serving.
- **The evidence has clear limits.** EPA, DHA and vitamin B12 in the panel come from the salted chum-salmon ikura table. That table does not report total choline or a phospholipid-class distribution. Astaxanthin is present in salmon roe; its amount in a particular commercial product is not established without product-relevant analysis.
- **Lemon and hot sauce primarily improve flavour and acceptability** at these quantities. No cognitive, treatment or clinical outcome can be inferred from one serving.

## Chilli and sensory neuroscience

Heston Blumenthal’s exploration of chilli and perception in *In Search of Perfection* provides a culinary context: in a [2016 first-person interview](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#hilleary_blumenthal_2016), he recalls filming the 2007 chilli episode and discussing how heat felt. Separately, [TRPV1 receptor experiments](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#caterina_capsaicin_1997) and [human oral-stimulation research](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#kawakami_capsaicin_2016) explain why small capsaicin exposures can produce noticeable sensations. The human study measured blood-oxygenation signals and fingertip temperature, not dopamine or endorphin release. This is not an endorsement of Neuroeshot.
