## Overview

Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) is a key brain phospholipid that serves as a precursor to both phosphatidylcholine (PC) and N-acyl phosphatidylethanolamines (NAPEs). NAPEs are precursors to N-acyl ethanolamines (NAEs) including palmitoylethanolamide (PEA), oleoylethanolamide (OEA), and anandamide (AEA), which are bioactive lipids that act as neuromodulators with anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and mood-regulating effects. PE has been investigated as a potential ADHD biomarker. Whether dietary PE makes a quantitatively meaningful contribution to neural NAPE/NAE precursor supply remains unresolved.

## References

- PE is a key brain phospholipid and potential ADHD biomarker; can convert to PC or N-acyl phosphatidylethanolamines (NAPEs)
- NAPEs are precursors to N-acyl ethanolamines (NAEs) like PEA, OEA, and anandamide, bioactive lipids with anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and mood-regulating effects
- PE’s intracellular precursor role does not establish that eating PE-containing foods increases neural NAPE/NAE availability or improves clinical outcomes.
- The single-arm oatmeal study NCT03468179 tested 10 participants after one weight-adjusted oatmeal serving, not an oat-bran or isolated-PE intervention. Its posted results report a mean serum NAPE change of −4.02 nmol/L (SE 10.57) at 120 minutes. NAE results were available for five participants; their mean was 0.09 nmol/L at baseline and 0.07 at 120 minutes, with five participants below detection. The proposed doubling was a study hypothesis, not a demonstrated result. These small, uncontrolled results do not establish dietary PE effects on the brain. [ClinicalTrials.gov posted results](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03468179?tab=results).
