## Overview

Glycine is a nonessential amino acid in food protein, including collagen and gelatin from cartilage, skin, and slow-cooked bone-in meats. The body can also make it. This page keeps that food constituent separate from glycine taken alone as a powder, tablet, or solution.

Glycine taken alone has its clearest nervous-system results in psychiatric illness, after weeks of high oral doses, and small high-bias trials reported better sleep after 3 g before bed [1]. A single much larger dose worsened sensorimotor gating and cognitive performance [1]. Glycine is also the amino acid added in the second step of glutathione assembly [2]. In older adults, glycine given with a cysteine precursor raised red-cell glutathione synthesis. That trial does not isolate glycine, and it did not measure the brain [3].

## Dietary Origin

Foods supply glycine inside protein. Collagen and gelatin are rich in it, which is why cartilage, skin, and broths from bone-in cuts are grouped here as dietary sources. The human trials gave glycine by itself. In the psychiatric studies that was about 0.2–0.8 g per kilogram of body weight per day for 6–12 weeks. In the healthy sleep studies it was 3 g, taken 30–60 minutes before bed, for two to four days [1]. The glutathione result used glycine together with a cysteine precursor, not a serving of collagen-rich food [3].

## Research Spotlights & Evidence Checks

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Does glycine improve sleep in healthy adults?"
  exposureContext="isolated-substance"
  summary="Three small trials reported better sleep quality, alertness, and cognition, and less fatigue and sleepiness, after 3 g of glycine before bed for two to four days. The review rates that evidence as high risk of bias [1]."
>

The trials were in healthy adults, or in women dissatisfied with their sleep, and used flavoured glycine rather than food [1]. Sample sizes were about 10–19 people. The reviewers treat the healthy-adult sleep, fatigue, and alertness findings cautiously because of those sizes and the high risk of bias [1]. A separate four-week study in overactive bladder reported shorter sleep latency at 3 g twice a day. That is a different population and dose from the healthy bedtime trials [1].

A single oral dose of about 0.8 g per kilogram, or an intravenous dose of 200 mg per kilogram, worsened sensorimotor gating and cognitive performance in healthy adults [1]. The bedtime finding and that large single dose are different exposures.

</EvidencePresentation>

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Do the psychiatric findings apply to ordinary food?"
  exposureContext="isolated-substance"
  summary="The clearer symptom changes were in people with psychiatric illness, using high oral doses for 6–12 weeks. The review found limited evidence that glycine prevents nervous-system decline in healthy adults [1]."
>

Fifteen of 18 studies in illness reported a positive nervous-system change, most clearly for psychiatric symptoms in schizophrenia and related conditions, and in some of those studies for cognition or extrapyramidal symptoms [1]. The oral doses were about 0.2–0.8 g per kilogram of body weight per day, continued for 6–12 weeks. The reviewers suggest these effects may involve the NMDA receptor, and they say that explanation still needs testing [1]. The same review found limited evidence for a preventative effect in healthy people, and it calls for larger trials before glycine is used to prevent age-related decline [1].

</EvidencePresentation>

## References

[1] Soh et al. (2024). [The effect of glycine administration on the characteristics of physiological systems in human adults: A systematic review](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#soh_glycine_administration_2024). Review of glycine given by itself to adults. Nervous-system changes were clearest in psychiatric illness at high oral doses for several weeks. Healthy-adult sleep findings used 3 g before bed for a few nights and were small studies at high risk of bias. A large single dose worsened sensorimotor gating and cognitive performance. Combined supplements were outside the review.

[2] Polekhina et al. (1999). [Molecular basis of glutathione synthetase deficiency and a rare gene permutation event](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#polekhina_gss_1999). Human glutathione synthetase adds glycine in the second step of glutathione assembly and binds one magnesium ion per subunit. Enzyme structure, not a food trial.

[3] Sekhar et al. (2011). [Deficient synthesis of glutathione underlies oxidative stress in aging and can be corrected by dietary cysteine and glycine supplementation](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#sekhar_glutathione_2011). Older adults had lower red-cell glutathione synthesis than younger adults. Two weeks of N-acetylcysteine together with glycine raised that synthesis. The trial does not isolate glycine, does not measure the brain, and is not an ordinary-food result.

[4] Lyons et al. (2000). [Blood glutathione synthesis rates in healthy adults receiving a sulfur amino acid-free diet](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#lyons_glutathione_2000). A diet containing neither methionine nor cysteine slowed whole-blood glutathione synthesis in healthy young men. Glycine in that formula was increased, so the slowdown is not a glycine deficit. Whole blood, not brain.
