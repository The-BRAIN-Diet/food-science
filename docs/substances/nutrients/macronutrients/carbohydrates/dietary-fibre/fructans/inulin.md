## Overview

Inulin is the longer-chain member of the inulin-type fructans, linear fructose polymers with a degree of polymerisation often described around 10–60. [Oligofructose](/docs/substances/nutrients/macronutrients/carbohydrates/dietary-fibre/fructans/oligofructose) is the short-chain mixture, about 2–8 or, in another convention, up to 10. Oligofructose-enriched inulin is a blend of the two. The names are not interchangeable [1,2].

In mice fed a Western diet, oligofructose-enriched inulin prevented the increase in inner-colonic mucus penetrability. Mucus growth was not restored [3]. The microbial effector was not isolated, and combining the fibre with Bifidobacterium longum NCC2705 did not preserve the penetrability effect [3]. That is a defined mouse exposure of the enriched mixture. It is not an indispensable nutrient, not a human dose, and not a result for inulin alone or for oligofructose alone [1,3].

## Dietary Origin

Chicory and some other plants store inulin-type fructans across a range of chain lengths. A food that contains mixed fructans does not, by that fact, contain a standardised long-chain inulin ingredient or the enriched blend used in the mouse diet [1]. Commercial oligofructose is usually a partial hydrolysate of chicory inulin and is a different preparation [1,2]. No food page here has a chain-length measurement that would support a contains relationship for inulin.

## Research Spotlights & Evidence Checks

  title="Does the mouse mucus result belong to inulin alone?"

The mouse study prevented increased inner-colonic mucus penetrability on a Western diet [3]. The material was the enriched blend, which Roberfroid separates from both longer-chain inulin and short-chain oligofructose [1]. The result cannot be reassigned to either fraction alone, or to a serving of chicory, onion, or garlic [1,3].
