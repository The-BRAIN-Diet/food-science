## Overview

Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential water-soluble vitamin used by methionine synthase in one-carbon metabolism and by methylmalonyl-CoA mutase in propionate and odd-chain-fatty-acid metabolism. These roles connect B12 status to homocysteine remethylation, myelin maintenance and neurological function [1,4]. Within BRAIN, B12 is treated as a required nutrient and PM substrate/cofactor relationship, not as a general cognitive enhancer.

Deficiency can produce haematological and neurological consequences, but deficiency treatment, ordinary dietary adequacy and supplementation in replete people are different evidence contexts [4]. Homocysteine-lowering studies have generally tested supplemental B vitamins, sometimes alongside omega-3 status, rather than whole B12-containing foods [1–3].

## Dietary Origin

Vitamin B12 occurs directly in animal-derived foods including shellfish, fish, meat, eggs and dairy, and in fortified foods. This is the ordinary relationship `Food → contains → Vitamin B12`; it does not require a microbial-formation or dietary-precursor module.

Some algae and fermented products contain corrinoids, but analytical detection alone does not establish human-active cobalamin. Chlorella has evidence for bioavailable B12 forms, whereas spirulina can be dominated by inactive analogues [5]. People who exclude animal foods generally require reliable fortified-food or supplemental sources.

## Research Spotlights & Evidence Checks

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Do homocysteine-lowering supplement trials establish an effect of B12-containing foods?"
  exposureContext="combination-formulation"
  summary="No. Trials combining folate, B12 and other nutrients support biochemical interaction in one-carbon metabolism, but they do not isolate a whole-food effect or prove that one component caused every observed outcome [1–3]."
>

The evidence supports B12 as a biochemical cofactor and shows that supplemental combinations can change homocysteine under studied conditions. It must not be translated into an isolated B12 food-outcome claim without direct evidence [1–3].

[4] Pawlak et al. (2013). [How prevalent is vitamin B12 deficiency among vegetarians?](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#pawlak_how_2013). Supports dietary-risk and deficiency context for B12 while distinguishing deficiency consequences from enhancement claims.
