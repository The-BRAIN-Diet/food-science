## Overview

Retinoic acid on this page is all-trans-retinoic acid, the acid metabolite made from [vitamin A](/docs/substances/nutrients/micronutrients/vitamins/vitamin-a). The structure shown is that isomer (C20H28O2). Foods are not recorded here as containing it. They supply preformed vitamin A, mainly retinol and retinyl esters, or provitamin A carotenoids such as beta-carotene. After absorption those precursors are oxidised to retinoic acid, and that last step is irreversible [1,2].

Beta-carotene can be cleaved to retinal before that oxidation [2]. The acid then acts as a local signal and is cleared by CYP26 enzymes, so a circulating vitamin A measurement is not a tissue retinoic acid measurement [1]. A serving of liver, egg yolk, butter or a carotenoid vegetable is a precursor exposure. Tretinoin is the drug name for this same molecule at a medicinal dose. The 9-cis and 13-cis isomers are different structures and are not this page.

## Dietary Origin

The foods below are linked as precursors, not as foods that contain retinoic acid. Animal foods in this set are dominated by preformed vitamin A. Leafy greens, carrots and sweet potatoes are dominated by beta-carotene. Figures are the per-100 g panels already on those food pages.

Preformed vitamin A: beef liver 4,968 µg RAE; ghee (anhydrous butter oil) 840 µg RAE; unsalted butter 684 µg RAE; egg yolk 381 µg RAE. Grass-fed butter uses that same unsalted-butter panel, so it is not a separate vitamin A assay. Beta-carotene: sweet potato 8,509 µg and 709 µg RAE; carrots 8,285 µg and 835 µg RAE; spinach 5,626 µg; romaine lettuce 5,226 µg; Swiss chard 3,647 µg; kale 2,873 µg. Many other foods record a trace of vitamin A or beta-carotene. Those traces are not linked here.

## Research Spotlights & Evidence Checks

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Do these foods contain retinoic acid?"
  exposureContext="food-constituent"
  summary="No. They supply retinol, retinyl esters or beta-carotene. Retinoic acid is formed afterwards by oxidation, and that step is irreversible [1,2]."
>

Intestinal absorption covers dietary vitamin A and provitamin A carotenoids. Beta-carotene can be cleaved to retinal, and retinal can be oxidised to retinoic acid [1,2]. That sequence is why the foods are dietary precursors. It is not a measurement of retinoic acid in the food, and a vitamin A blood value does not stand in for the acid in a tissue [1].

</EvidencePresentation>

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Is a tretinoin medicine the same exposure as these foods?"
  exposureContext="supplemental-formulation"
  summary="No. Tretinoin is a medicinal dose of all-trans-retinoic acid. A food portion is the precursor, not that dose [1]."
>

The drug uses the same all-trans molecule this page identifies. A prescribed dose is a different exposure from liver, egg yolk, butter or a carotenoid vegetable. High-dose oral retinoid medicines are teratogenic; that warning belongs to the medicine and to excess preformed vitamin A, not to a recipe. This page does not set a dietary requirement for retinoic acid.

</EvidencePresentation>

## References

[1] Duester (2008). [Retinoic acid synthesis and signaling during early organogenesis](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#duester_retinoic_acid_2008). Retinol is oxidised to retinaldehyde and then to retinoic acid; the second step is irreversible, and CYP26 enzymes clear the acid. Tissue signalling is local, not a circulating vitamin A result.

[2] Harrison (2012). [Mechanisms involved in the intestinal absorption of dietary vitamin A and provitamin A carotenoids](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#harrison_vitamin_a_absorption_2012). Dietary vitamin A is absorbed as retinol and retinyl esters. Provitamin A carotenoids such as beta-carotene can be cleaved to retinal. The review is about absorption and cleavage, not a measurement of retinoic acid in food.
