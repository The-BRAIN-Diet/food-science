## Overview

Taurine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that is not built into proteins. It occurs free in cells and is most abundant in the heart, retina, developing brain and blood. The liver makes some taurine from methionine and cysteine, but humans have low activity of the enzyme cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase, so most taurine comes from food [1]. In experimental models taurine acts as an inhibitory neuromodulator at GABA-A, GABA-B and glycine receptors and helps limit calcium overload [2]. In humans, randomised trials have not shown that taurine supplements reliably improve cognition [3,4]. A 2 g dose did not change salivary cortisol [5].

## Dietary Context

### Food sources

Diet is the main human source of taurine [1]. Shellfish carry the most: mean raw values of about 828 mg per 100 g for scallops, 655 mg for mussels and up to 521 mg for clams. Dark poultry meat follows, with 306 mg per 100 g for raw dark turkey and 83–170 mg for raw dark chicken, against about 18–30 mg for light meat. Dairy foods carry little [1]. Cooking does not appear to reduce taurine content [1]. Animal-sourced foods are the main dietary sources, so plant-based patterns supply less [4]. The Foods list records foods that carry a taurine relationship.

### Supplement versus food

Estimated intake for adult non-vegetarians is about 40–400 mg per day, typically nearer the lower end [1]. The human trials discussed here used 1–3 g single doses [4] or 4 g a day for 12 weeks (see [Therapeutic Area Research](/docs/substances/bioactive-compounds/taurine?therapeutic=1#taurine-psychosis)), many times what food supplies. None of the acute cognition trials measured habitual diet or baseline taurine status [4]. Their results do not show what ordinary dietary taurine does.

### Synergies and interactions

Energy drinks combine taurine with caffeine. When the two were tested separately, the cognitive changes after an energy-drink-style dose followed caffeine rather than taurine [5]. Combination trials do not isolate taurine's contribution [4]. Endogenous synthesis depends on pyridoxal 5′-phosphate, the active form of vitamin B6, at two steps of the cysteine route [1].

## Research Spotlights & Evidence Checks

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Does taurine supplementation improve cognition?"
  exposureContext="supplemental-formulation"
  summary="Not reliably. A meta-analysis of 7 randomised trials found no overall cognitive effect [3], and a review of 8 acute trials in healthy adults found small, inconsistent effects at best [4]."
>

Cao and colleagues pooled 7 randomised controlled trials with 9 intervention arms and 402 participants [3]. Taurine alone, or combined with exercise, did not improve cognitive scores. Added to therapeutic drugs, it gave no overall gain over the drugs alone. One subgroup, Mini-Mental State Examination scores with taurine plus drugs, improved (weighted mean difference 3.09, 95% CI 2.29–3.90). The authors concluded that the evidence does not support taurine for improving cognitive function.

Moore and colleagues reviewed 8 acute, single-dose randomised trials in 244 healthy adults aged 18–45, using typically 1–3 g of taurine [4]. Most cognitive outcomes showed no effect. Trials that combined taurine with caffeine could not isolate taurine, and mood effects were minor and context-dependent. They concluded that acute taurine cannot currently be recommended as a reliable cognitive or mood enhancer.

</EvidencePresentation>

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Does taurine lower cortisol?"
  exposureContext="isolated-substance"
  summary="Not in the human trial that measured it: 2 g of taurine did not change salivary cortisol in a randomised, double-blind study [5]."
>

Giles and colleagues gave 48 habitual caffeine consumers, after 24 hours without caffeine, 2,000 mg taurine, 200 mg caffeine, both, or neither on separate days [5]. Salivary cortisol was measured, and no treatment changed it. Taurine alone produced mixed reaction-time results and reversed caffeine's effect on vigour. The authors attributed energy-drink cognitive effects to caffeine, not taurine. This single acute trial does not test chronic stress, but it gives no human support for a cortisol-lowering or adaptogenic effect.

</EvidencePresentation>

<h2 id="taurine-advanced-nutrition" tabIndex={-1}>Advanced Nutrition</h2>

<p>This panel describes how taurine is made and what experimental work reports about its actions. Preclinical mechanisms do not establish a dietary dose or a clinical effect.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-synthesis">From cysteine to taurine</h3>

<p>Hepatic synthesis begins with methionine, which forms homocysteine and then cystathionine. A pyridoxal 5′-phosphate step yields cysteine. Cysteine dioxygenase oxidises cysteine to cysteine sulfinic acid, which cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase, again with pyridoxal 5′-phosphate, converts to hypotaurine. Hypotaurine is oxidised to taurine. A minor route runs through cysteic acid <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-1">[1]</a>.</p>

<p>Humans have low cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase activity, which is why food supplies most taurine. Absorbed taurine enters cells through the taurine transporter. When body taurine is high the transporter is downregulated and the kidneys excrete the excess. When it is low, renal reabsorption rises <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-1">[1]</a>.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-plant-foods">Plant foods</h3>

<p>Laidlaw and colleagues measured 29 meats, 9 dairy products and 48 plant foods, including vegetables, nuts, seeds, fruits and legumes. Taurine was detected in meats and dairy products but not in the plant foods <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-2">[2]</a>.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-receptors-calcium">Receptor and calcium actions</h3>

<p>In experimental systems taurine opens chloride channels and hyperpolarises neurons, acting on GABA-A, GABA-B and glycine receptors. Its GABA-A activity raises GABA levels and the expression of the GABA-synthesising enzymes GAD65 and GAD67 <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-3">[3]</a>. Taurine also limits calcium overload: in cultured neurons it reduced glutamate-induced excitotoxicity by restraining calcium influx through the reverse mode of the sodium–calcium exchanger <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-3">[3]</a>. Jangra and colleagues summarise the same inhibitory, receptor-agonist and calcium-modulating profile <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-4">[4]</a>.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-mitochondria">Mitochondrial protection</h3>

<p>Excess intracellular calcium triggers the mitochondrial permeability transition, which releases pro-apoptotic factors. Taurine's protection against calcium overload is one route by which it limits this injury in models <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-3">[3]</a>. Taurine also supports expression of ND6, a respiratory complex I subunit, with higher complex I activity, lower lactic acidosis and lower reactive oxygen species in experimental work <a href="#taurine-advanced-ref-4">[4]</a>. These are model findings. They do not show a bioenergetic effect from dietary taurine in humans.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-psychosis">First-episode psychosis</h3>

<p>O'Donnell and colleagues randomised 121 people aged 18–25 with first-episode psychosis who were taking low-dose antipsychotic medication to taurine 4 g a day or placebo for 12 weeks <a href="#taurine-ta-ref-1">[1]</a>. Eighty-six were analysed (47 taurine, 39 placebo). Taurine improved Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale total scores (P = .004) and the psychotic subscale (P = .026), with smaller improvements in the Calgary Depression Scale for Schizophrenia (P = .047) and Global Assessment of Functioning (P = .04). The co-primary cognitive outcome, the MATRICS composite, did not differ (P = .582).</p>

<p>This is one phase 2 trial of a pharmacological dose added to medication. The authors called for larger trials. It does not show that dietary taurine affects psychosis.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-dementia">Dementia association and Alzheimer's mouse work</h3>

<p>Chouraki and colleagues measured 217 plasma metabolites in 2,067 dementia-free Framingham Offspring participants (mean age 55.9) and followed them for a mean of 15.6 years; 93 developed dementia <a href="#taurine-ta-ref-2">[2]</a>. Each standard-deviation increase in plasma taurine was associated with lower all-cause dementia risk (hazard ratio 0.74, 95% CI 0.60–0.92, p = 0.007). This met the study's "suggestive" threshold but not its multiple-testing threshold. For Alzheimer's disease alone the association was no longer apparent (hazard ratio 0.91). The authors suggested a vascular rather than Alzheimer-specific route.</p>

<p>Kim and colleagues gave 7-month-old APP/PS1 mice taurine in drinking water at 1,000 mg/kg/day for 6 weeks <a href="#taurine-ta-ref-3">[3]</a>. Spatial working memory and passive-avoidance memory recovered to wild-type levels. Plaque number, area and size did not change, soluble amyloid-β was largely unaltered, and insoluble cortical amyloid-β42 fell only slightly. The cognitive effect was therefore not explained by plaque reduction.</p>

<p>Cao and colleagues found no overall cognitive benefit of taurine in randomised trials, including trials adding taurine to therapeutic drugs; an MMSE subgroup did improve <a href="#taurine-ta-ref-4">[4]</a>. No adequately powered trial of taurine alone in people with dementia is cited here.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-depression">Depression biomarkers</h3>

<p>Altamura and colleagues found higher plasma taurine in 25 unmedicated patients with major depression than in 22 controls, with no relationship to severity <a href="#taurine-ta-ref-5">[5]</a>. A later meta-analysis of 46 studies found lower taurine in major depression, and lower taurine in the antidepressant-free subgroup <a href="#taurine-ta-ref-6">[6]</a>. The direction of the difference is inconsistent, and neither finding shows that taurine intake treats depression.</p>

<h3 id="taurine-adhd">ADHD</h3>

<p>Jakaria and colleagues summarise rat work in an ADHD model in which a high taurine dose lowered locomotion in spontaneously hypertensive rats while a low dose increased it <a href="#taurine-ta-ref-7">[7]</a>. These are mixed animal results. No human ADHD trial of taurine is cited here.</p>
