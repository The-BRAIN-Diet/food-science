## Overview

Acetic acid (ethanoic acid) is the characteristic acid of vinegar. The structure image is the neutral acid, InChIKey `QTBSBXVTEAMEQO-UHFFFAOYSA-N`. [Acetate](/docs/substances/microbial-metabolites/scfas/acetate) is the conjugate base recorded as a microbial fermentation metabolite. A vinegar meal is not that colonic metabolite, and faecal acetate measurements are not vinegar exposure.

The admitted BRS6 practice is [vinegar with the studied starchy meal](/docs/biological-targets/brs6/fm1/brs6-fm1-pm1-glucose-appearance-kinetics#pm1-f6). In healthy volunteers, vinegar with a macronutrient-matched bread meal lowered the glycaemic and insulin indices and the paracetamol emptying-marker response. The marker is indirect. Meal-glucose appearance was not measured [1]. Further emptying delay in diabetic gastroparesis may be disadvantageous [2].

## Dietary Context

### Food sources

Direct food records are [vinegar](/docs/foods/vinegar) and [vinegar pickles](/docs/foods/vinegar-pickles#acetic-acid-exposure). Cider vinegar is identified by acetic acid. FDA labeling guidance says this vinegar should contain 4 g acetic acid per 100 mL at 20 °C, and that no vinegar standard of identity has been set under the FD&C Act. That figure is not a measured amount for the USDA cider-vinegar record, so the food page does not store it as a per-100 g value [3].

Vinegar pickles carry acetic acid from the vinegar brine. The USDA dill-pickle record does not report how much acetic acid is in the edible portion. Portion size and whether the brine is eaten change exposure [3].

### Supplement versus food

The gastric-delivery evidence is vinegar taken with a starchy meal, not an acetic acid capsule or a measured acid dose separated from the vinegar [1,2]. Containing acetic acid does not mean a pickle portion reproduces that meal comparison.

## Research Spotlights & Evidence Checks

<EvidencePresentation
  kind="check"
  title="Does the vinegar meal study isolate acetic acid?"
  exposureContext="whole-food"
  summary="No. The admitted comparison is vinegar with a matched starchy bread meal. Acetic acid was not given as an isolated compound, and meal-glucose appearance was not measured [1,2]."
>

Ten healthy volunteers ate macronutrient-matched bread meals with and without vinegar. Relative glycaemic and insulin indices were 64 and 65, and the paracetamol response was lower, which is consistent with delayed emptying. The marker is indirect, the trial is small and acute, and no tracer measured appearance [1]. A separate ultrasound pilot in ten people with type 1 diabetes and gastroparesis found further emptying delay with apple cider vinegar. That result limits the practice. It is not a beneficial entry-rate intervention for that group [2].

</EvidencePresentation>
