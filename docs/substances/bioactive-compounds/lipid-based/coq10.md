## Overview

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is a lipid-soluble redox carrier synthesised endogenously and also present in foods. **Ubiquinone-10** is its oxidised form and **ubiquinol-10** its reduced form; total CoQ10 measurements may combine both. Unspecified “ubiquinone” does not identify a side-chain length. CoQ9 is a different homologue, whereas COQ9 is a biosynthetic protein. The displayed InChIKey identifies ubiquinone-10, not ubiquinol-10. [1]

Production and distribution are described in [Coenzyme Q Biosynthesis & Pool Availability](/docs/biological-targets/brs4/fm1/brs4-fm1-pm10-coenzyme-q-biosynthesis-and-pool-availability). CoQ pool abundance, redox fraction and mitochondrial respiration are distinct. Oral supplementation may increase plasma concentrations without increasing measured muscle or mitochondrial CoQ. This does not establish universal ineffectiveness or equivalence between formulations. [2, 3]

Ageing does not establish a universal 50% decline in tissue CoQ. A cross-sectional serum study found different age patterns for total and oxidised CoQ, with the cholesterol-adjusted total pattern not statistically significant. Sample matrix and ex-vivo oxidation affect interpretation; a maximally reduced pool is not a universal goal. [4]

## Foods

Measured olive-oil results were 56 ± 4 mg/L for seven commercial extra-virgin blends, 52 ± 17 mg/L for 79 fresh/fresh-frozen monocultivar samples, and 54 ± 10 mg/L for two refined-oil samples. These are sample-group measurements, not universal values; no density-based conversion to mg/100 g or native ubiquinol fraction is inferred. [5]

Six named pistachio samples contained 10.21–42.16 µg/g CoQ10 by nano-liquid chromatography. This does not establish one value for all cultivars or roasted kernels. [7]

CoQ occurs in analysed olive oils and animal tissues. Olive cultivar and maturity affect measured content; fresh extra-virgin samples and commercial blends cannot supply one universal concentration. Existing beef and fish records retain their source-material and preparation qualifications. Neither presence nor a source-specific concentration establishes mitochondrial delivery. No organ-meat quantity is attributed to the olive-oil study. [5, 6]

<h2 id="coq10-adhd">CoQ10 and ADHD — preliminary clinical evidence</h2>

<Collapse title="Adjunctive CoQ10 and parent-rated ADHD symptoms">

Gamal et al. (2022) randomised 60 children with persistent symptoms despite atomoxetine to adjunctive CoQ10 or placebo for six months. Parent-rated CPRS-48 total scores improved more with CoQ10 at six months: reported mean percentage reductions were 33.87% versus 18.24%. These are score changes, not recovery rates. This is a clinical symptom signal in the studied adjunctive-treatment setting. [1]

The author proof reports 1–3 mg/kg/dose CoQ10 but does not clearly specify dosing frequency or formulation in its methods; the trial registry does not resolve all protocol inconsistencies. This is not a dosing recommendation. The total-score difference was not significant at one month. Three-month prose and Table 3 disagree about significance. Adverse-event counts also conflict between text and table; reduced adverse effects are not established. [1,2]

**Evidence limitations:** One small, single-centre dataset; parent-rated outcomes; age bounds, registry endpoint/enrolment and reported tables require reconciliation. Independent clinical SEC appraisal remains pending for bias protection and precision; no completed numerical score is assigned. The reported six-month benefit is retained rather than treated as evidence of no effect.

**Mechanism boundary:** The study did not measure mitochondrial CoQ10 pools, biosynthetic flux, energetic reserve or allostatic load. It does not establish monotherapy efficacy, equivalent benefit from ordinary food intake, or mitochondrial mediation of symptom improvement.
