## Overview

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is a lipid-soluble redox carrier synthesised endogenously and also present in foods. **Ubiquinone-10** is its oxidised form and **ubiquinol-10** its reduced form; total CoQ10 measurements may combine both. Unspecified “ubiquinone” does not identify a side-chain length. CoQ9 is a different homologue, whereas COQ9 is a biosynthetic protein. The displayed InChIKey identifies ubiquinone-10, not ubiquinol-10. [1]

Production and distribution are described in [Coenzyme Q Biosynthesis & Pool Availability](/docs/biological-targets/brs4/fm1/brs4-fm1-pm10-coenzyme-q-biosynthesis-and-pool-availability). CoQ pool abundance, redox fraction and mitochondrial respiration are distinct. Oral supplementation may increase plasma concentrations without increasing measured muscle or mitochondrial CoQ. This does not establish universal ineffectiveness or equivalence between formulations. [2, 3]

Ageing does not establish a universal 50% decline in tissue CoQ. A cross-sectional serum study found different age patterns for total and oxidised CoQ, with the cholesterol-adjusted total pattern not statistically significant. Sample matrix and ex-vivo oxidation affect interpretation; a maximally reduced pool is not a universal goal. [4]

## Foods

<SubstanceFoods tag="CoQ10" />

CoQ occurs in analysed olive oils and animal tissues. Olive cultivar and maturity affect measured content; fresh extra-virgin samples and commercial blends cannot supply one universal concentration. Existing beef and fish records retain their source-material and preparation qualifications. Neither presence nor a source-specific concentration establishes mitochondrial delivery. No organ-meat quantity is attributed to the olive-oil study. [5, 6]
