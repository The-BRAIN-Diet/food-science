## Overview

Choline is an essential nutrient. The liver does not synthesise enough to meet requirements, so intake depends on food and, when diet falls short, supplements [1]. Eggs, meat and milk dominate surveyed intakes; some plants such as broccoli contribute smaller amounts [1]. This page treats dietary choline as a food constituent — not as a proxy for lecithin trials, phosphatidylcholine–DHA transport studies, or cholinergic-receptor work.

Derbyshire (2019) places choline in neurotransmitter synthesis, membrane phospholipids, methylation via glycine betaine, liver function and homocysteine regulation. IOM and EFSA have set adequate intakes; most surveyed European, US, Canadian and Australian populations fall below them, and UK databases still omit choline [1]. Derbyshire and Maes (2023) report low or altered choline status in pregnancy and in some people with autism spectrum conditions, ADHD or dyslexia — a possible modifiable risk factor, not a treatment trial [2]. EFSA authorised lipid, liver and homocysteine claims in 2011; neurological-function evidence was not judged sufficient then [1].

## Key Compound Highlights

- Essential: endogenous synthesis does not meet human requirements [1].
- Oxidised choline (glycine betaine) contributes to S-adenosylmethionine for DNA and histone methylation [1].
- Eggs, meat and milk are the main dietary providers; further restriction of those foods can lower intake [1].
- Most surveyed European, American, Canadian and Australian populations miss adequate-intake thresholds; UK composition databases and guidelines still omit choline [1].
- Neurodevelopmental narrative: low or altered status reported in pregnancy and in some people with ASC, ADHD or dyslexia — not an ADHD treatment trial [2].
- EFSA 2011 authorised claims for lipid metabolism, liver function and homocysteine reduction; neurological-function evidence was not judged sufficient [1].

## Dietary Context

### Food sources

Animal foods contain more choline per unit weight than plant sources. Beef, eggs, fish, chicken, nuts, milk and some plants such as broccoli contribute; eggs, dairy and meat dominate in the intake surveys summarised by Derbyshire [1]. Egg consumers in US NHANES data had almost twice the usual choline intake of non-consumers [1].

### Synergies

One-carbon metabolism is shared with folate and related B vitamins; UK survey practice still reports those B vitamins and not choline [1]. Oestrogen status and PEMT genotype can raise choline requirements above the general adequate intake [1]. Phosphatidylcholine–DHA transport across the blood–brain barrier is a separate phosphatidylcholine story and is not attributed here.

### Supplement versus food

US survey analyses found that meeting the adequate intake was difficult without eggs or a supplement [1]. If food does not supply enough, Derbyshire flags supplementation especially in pregnancy and lactation [1]. Lecithin is a concentrated phosphatidylcholine source, not equivalent to the egg or meat matrix. Food-page choline listings are compositional.
