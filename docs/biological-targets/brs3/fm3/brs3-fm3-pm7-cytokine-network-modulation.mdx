### Overview

In the two trials cited here, one EPA+DHA intervention showed no significant between-group effect on the individual circulating cytokines assessed, while the other reduced IL‑6 production in stimulated blood cells. [[2]](#pm-ref-2) [[4]](#pm-ref-4) A separate trial in adults with chronic venous leg ulcers found lower circulating IL‑6, IL‑1β and TNF‑α after a defined EPA+DHA protocol (Tan et al., 2018). [[10]](#pm-ref-10)

Evidence from omega‑3 supplementation trials suggests possible reductions in post-exercise muscle soreness and selected inflammatory and muscle-damage markers. Whether the cytokine changes contributed to recovery or extend to resting inflammatory tone remains unclear. [[12]](#pm-ref-12)

IL-6 and TNF-α are inflammatory messengers. Slavich and Irwin (2014) place them on the path from stress-related immune signalling to inflammatory tone. [[5]](#pm-ref-5) Ferguson et al. (2014) used an endotoxin challenge in healthy adults to read that response. [[2]](#pm-ref-2) EPA plus DHA did not significantly change IL-6 or TNF-α in that challenge. [[2]](#pm-ref-2) Ferguson et al. measured plasma CRP as a marker of systemic inflammation, alongside the circulating cytokines IL‑6 and TNF‑α. [[2]](#pm-ref-2)

- **Benefits:** PM7 focuses on signalling through inflammatory messengers such as IL-6 and TNF-α. [[5]](#pm-ref-5)
- **Implementation Notes:** Capó measured cytokine production in stimulated blood cells after exercise. Tan measured circulating levels with both arms receiving low-dose aspirin. The companion analysis identifies the tested capsule composition. [[8]](#pm-ref-8) [[10]](#pm-ref-10) [[11]](#pm-ref-11)
- **Biological Relevance:** [PM1](/docs/biological-targets/brs3/fm1/brs3-fm1-pm1-nf-kb-signalling-regulation) covers NF-κB-mediated transcription. PM7 covers cytokine signalling.

<strong>Fermented foods, gut microbes and inflammation</strong>

<div className="brs-fm-hub-panel" hidden>

In a 2021 study, Wastyk and colleagues asked whether changing what people ate could affect their gut microbiome and immune function. They compared two dietary approaches. One group increased its intake of fermented foods such as yoghurt, kefir and fermented vegetables. The other group increased its fibre intake.

In the fermented-food group, gut microbes became more diverse and levels of 19 of the 93 inflammation-related proteins measured in blood fell during the intervention. These were exploratory findings. The study’s primary measure of how immune cells responded to stimulation showed no significant change.

The study measured the gut microbiome and blood proteins separately. Seeing both change in the same group raises a question about how the two responses are connected. One way to explore that connection is to compare individual results. Did the participants whose gut microbes changed the most also show the largest falls in a specific inflammatory protein, such as IL‑6?

[Read Wastyk et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2021.06.019)
