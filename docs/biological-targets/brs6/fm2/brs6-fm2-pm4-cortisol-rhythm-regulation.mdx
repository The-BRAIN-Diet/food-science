summary: 'Cortisol follows a daily pattern, highest in the morning and lowest late in the evening. Controlled studies show that behaviour can move that pattern: shifting sleep and meals against the body clock reversed it, sleep loss raised evening cortisol, and an earlier eating window lowered it. Light raises or lowers cortisol depending on timing. Youths with ADHD show lower morning cortisol, but the size of the difference is uncertain and no study has tested correcting it.'

    finding_label: Misalignment reverses cortisol timing relative to the day
    presentation: mechanistic-basis
    fm_rollup: false
    finding_summary: Living about twelve hours out of step with the body clock reversed the daily cortisol pattern relative to the behavioural day.
    finding_statement: Forced circadian misalignment of sleep and meals by about twelve hours completely reversed the daily cortisol pattern relative to the behavioural schedule.
    finding_interpretation: 'This is the only study on this page that measured the cortisol rhythm itself, with repeated sampling across the day, rather than levels at selected times.'
    synthesis: 'In a ten-day forced-desynchrony protocol, cortisol stayed locked to the body clock while sleep and meals moved around it, so when behaviour was about twelve hours misaligned, cortisol peaked during the behavioural night and fell during the behavioural day. The same misalignment lowered leptin, raised glucose despite higher insulin and reduced sleep efficiency. The experiment establishes that the relationship between cortisol timing and daily behaviour can be disrupted by behaviour; it does not show which cue was responsible or how to restore an already disrupted rhythm.'
    synthesis_limitations: 'One small laboratory experiment in ten healthy adults under an extreme schedule. Sleep, meals and light moved together, so their separate contributions were not isolated. No restoration, dose or ordinary schedule variation was tested.'

    presentation: mechanistic-basis

    finding_statement: 'Bright light acutely changes cortisol, but the effect depends on timing: light after waking or in the early morning raised cortisol, light in the afternoon or evening had no effect, and several hours of very bright light across the biological night and morning lowered it.'
    finding_interpretation: 'Light acts on adrenal output acutely. Whether repeated daily light shifts the cortisol rhythm was not tested, and the opposite directions mean that light cannot be described as simply raising or lowering cortisol.'
    synthesis: 'Three laboratory experiments agree that light affects cortisol only at certain times. One hour of 800 lux after waking raised salivary cortisol by about 35% compared with dim light, while evening light had no effect. After a night awake, moving from dim to bright light at 05:00 raised cortisol by more than 50% within minutes, while the same light at 13:00 did not. The direction therefore depends on when and how long light is given, and on the protocol.'
    synthesis_limitations: 'Small laboratory samples, mostly of men; acute endpoints over hours; extreme protocols (sleep deprivation, prolonged very bright light); no repeated daily exposure or measurement of rhythm timing or amplitude.'

  - id: PM4-F7
    finding_label: 'Youths with ADHD have lower morning cortisol, of uncertain size'
    presentation: mechanistic-basis
    fm_rollup: false
    therapeutic_area_ids:
      - TA001
    finding_summary: 'Youths with ADHD have lower morning cortisol than their peers, with no afternoon difference, but how large the gap is remains unsettled.'
    finding_statement: 'In youths with ADHD, basal salivary cortisol is lower than in typically developing youths, most consistently in the morning and in total daily output, with no difference in the afternoon. The size of the morning difference is uncertain.'
    finding_interpretation: 'The direction is consistent; the size is not settled. These studies measured cortisol at set times, so they do not show whether rhythm timing or amplitude differs, and they do not show whether higher morning cortisol would be better.'
    synthesis: 'A meta-analysis of 19 studies (916 youths with ADHD and 947 typically developing youths) found lower basal cortisol, lower total daily output and lower morning cortisol (g = 0.84), with no afternoon difference. Heterogeneity was extreme. In the authors'' sensitivity analysis, two studies drove the morning and basal effects; without them the morning effect fell to g = 0.24 and heterogeneity fell from 96% to 65.5%. Both studies sampled young children around a stressful event: a laboratory stress test in one, and a clinic visit before an intravenous line in the other, where most diagnoses were parent reported. In the clinic study, home samples at the same clock times were not lower. The largest single cohort found lower waking and 30-minute cortisol only above age 10. The direction therefore holds across studies, but the magnitude, and how it varies with age, diagnosis and sampling setting, is unresolved.'
    synthesis_limitations: 'All studies are cross-sectional case–control comparisons; no study tested causation or change over time. The studies on this page are not independent: four are included in the meta-analysis. No study measured rhythm timing or amplitude. Immunoassays also detect inactive cortisol metabolites, and waking samples depend on unverified timing at home. The largest cohort found no link between cortisol and symptom severity.'

## BRS6-2-4 - Cortisol Rhythm Regulation


## 1. Mission & Overview

### Mission

Maintain diurnal cortisol amplitude and phase so morning activation and evening downshift stay well timed.

**Intervention Dominance:** Lifestyle-Dominant (inherited qualification, retained pending approval). Principal route displayed: System Optimisation Practices. This placement reflects the assessed process, not proven comparative superiority.

### Overview

Cortisol follows a daily pattern, highest in the morning and falling to a quiet period late in the evening. Leproult et al. (1997) [[14]](#pm-ref-14) In controlled studies, living out of step with the body clock reversed that pattern, sleep loss slowed the evening fall and an earlier eating window lowered evening cortisol. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1); Leproult et al. (1997) [[14]](#pm-ref-14); Jamshed et al. (2019) [[16]](#pm-ref-16) Light raised or lowered cortisol depending on timing. Scheer and Buijs (1999) [[11]](#pm-ref-11); Jung et al. (2010) [[13]](#pm-ref-13)

- **Benefits:** A well-timed rhythm keeps cortisol high during the behavioural morning and low in the evening, in step with the day. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1)
- **Implementation Notes:** Aligned sleep–wake and meal scheduling: the evidence comes from shifting sleep and meals about twelve hours out of step, so it shows what disrupts the rhythm rather than defining an ideal timetable. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1)
- **Biological Relevance:** A higher or lower reading at one time of day is not the same as a better-timed rhythm; the rise after waking is a separate component. Schmidt et al. (2015) [[3]](#pm-ref-3)

## 2. Primary Biological Effects

- Forced misalignment of sleep and meals reversed the daily cortisol pattern relative to the behavioural schedule. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1)
- Sleep loss raised next-evening cortisol and delayed its evening fall. Leproult et al. (1997) [[14]](#pm-ref-14); Spiegel et al. (1999) [[15]](#pm-ref-15)
- An earlier eating window lowered evening cortisol; delaying meals alone did not shift the rhythm. Jamshed et al. (2019) [[16]](#pm-ref-16); Wehrens et al. (2017) [[7]](#pm-ref-7)
- Light after waking raised cortisol, while prolonged bright light at night lowered it. Scheer and Buijs (1999) [[11]](#pm-ref-11); Leproult et al. (2001) [[12]](#pm-ref-12); Jung et al. (2010) [[13]](#pm-ref-13)
- The studied B-GOS prebiotic lowered the cortisol rise after waking. Schmidt et al. (2015) [[3]](#pm-ref-3)

## 4. Mechanistic Basis + Scientific Findings

### Summary

The cortisol rhythm is set by the body clock and adjusted by behaviour and light. The first six mechanisms describe what moves cortisol timing or level; the seventh describes how the daily profile differs in youths with ADHD. Each mechanism has a matching Scientific Finding in section 4.1.

<div class="brs-fm-hub-item" data-brs-fm-hub>
<div class="brs-fm-hub-shell">
<button type="button" class="brs-fm-hub-summary" aria-expanded="false"><span class="brs-fm-hub-chevron" aria-hidden="true"></span><strong>Cortisol rhythm regulation — mechanistic detail</strong></button>
<div class="brs-fm-hub-panel" hidden>

<span id="pm4-mechanism-f1" />

#### Misalignment reverses cortisol timing relative to the day

Cortisol output follows the body clock rather than the behavioural schedule. When sleep and meals were moved about twelve hours out of step with the clock, cortisol peaked during the behavioural night and fell during the behavioural day, while glucose rose despite higher insulin. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1) Misalignment changes the relationship between the cortisol rhythm and the day it is meant to support.

Supporting evidence: [SF1](#pm4-f1).

<span id="pm4-mechanism-f2" />

#### A prebiotic changes the waking response, not a demonstrated full rhythm

The cortisol rise in the first hour after waking is a distinct segment of the daily curve. Three weeks of B-GOS lowered that rise in healthy volunteers, while FOS did not, so the effect is specific to the preparation. Schmidt et al. (2015) [[3]](#pm-ref-3) How a prebiotic would reach adrenal output was not shown, and the rest of the day was not sampled.

Supporting evidence: [SF2](#pm4-f2).

<span id="pm4-mechanism-f3" />

#### Delaying meals alone did not shift the cortisol rhythm

Meals set clocks in peripheral tissues. Moving meals five hours later delayed glucose rhythms and an adipose clock gene, but cortisol and melatonin phase stayed put when light and sleep were fixed. Wehrens et al. (2017) [[7]](#pm-ref-7) Meal timing alone therefore appears to be a weak signal for the central timing of cortisol.

Supporting evidence: [SF3](#pm4-f3).

<span id="pm4-mechanism-f4" />

#### An earlier eating window lowered evening cortisol in a short trial

Compressing eating into the morning lowered evening cortisol after four days at the same energy intake, with a trend toward higher morning values. Jamshed et al. (2019) [[16]](#pm-ref-16) This changed the level at the end of the day rather than a measured rhythm phase, and it is a different exposure from the meal delay above.

Supporting evidence: [SF4](#pm4-f4).

<span id="pm4-mechanism-f5" />

#### Light changes cortisol, with direction set by timing and protocol

Light reaches the adrenal glands through neural and endocrine pathways from the body clock. Jung et al. (2010) [[13]](#pm-ref-13) After waking or early in the morning, light added to the cortisol rise; in the afternoon or evening it had no effect. Scheer and Buijs (1999) [[11]](#pm-ref-11); Leproult et al. (2001) [[12]](#pm-ref-12) Prolonged very bright light across the biological night and morning lowered cortisol instead. Jung et al. (2010) [[13]](#pm-ref-13)

Supporting evidence: [SF5](#pm4-f5).

<span id="pm4-mechanism-f6" />

#### Sleep loss raises evening cortisol and delays its evening fall

Cortisol normally falls toward a quiet period in the evening. After sleep loss, evening cortisol stayed higher and the quiet period started later, with no change at other times of day; the authors propose slower recovery of the stress axis from its morning activation. Leproult et al. (1997) [[14]](#pm-ref-14) A week of short sleep also raised evening cortisol. Spiegel et al. (1999) [[15]](#pm-ref-15)

Supporting evidence: [SF6](#pm4-f6).

<span id="pm4-mechanism-f7" />

#### Youths with ADHD have lower morning cortisol, of uncertain size

The daily profile in youths with ADHD is lower in the morning and in total daily output, with no afternoon difference. Chang et al. (2021) [[2]](#pm-ref-2); Isaksson et al. (2012) [[4]](#pm-ref-4) The size of the gap depends on two studies of young children sampled around stressful events, one of which found no difference in home samples. Maldonado et al. (2009) [[8]](#pm-ref-8); McCarthy et al. (2011) [[9]](#pm-ref-9) This describes levels at set times; it does not show a shifted or flattened rhythm, or which direction of change would help.

Supporting evidence: [SF7](#pm4-f7).

#### Biological process

The daily cortisol curve combines a clock-driven rhythm with responses to waking, light, sleep and meals. Only the misalignment experiment measured rhythm timing directly; the other studies measured levels at selected times or over a few hours. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1)

#### Mechanism Boundary

This PM covers daily cortisol timing and amplitude. The cortisol response to an acute stressor is a different measurement, and autonomic stress recovery belongs to [BRS6(FM3) PM6](/docs/biological-targets/brs6/fm3/brs6-fm3-pm6-sympathetic-activation-and-parasympathetic-recovery). Entrainment of the body clock by light and meals belongs to the cross-BRS circadian mechanisms. The Mission describes the biological job; it does not assert that every tested lever achieves the entire Mission.

#### Integration

This PM contributes the timing of cortisol output to BRS6(FM2). It depends on clock entrainment upstream and connects to glucose regulation, since misalignment raised glucose despite higher insulin. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1)

### 4.1 Scientific Findings

#### Summary

Controlled experiments in healthy adults show that behaviour can move cortisol timing: misalignment reversed the daily pattern, sleep loss raised evening cortisol and an earlier eating window lowered it, while delaying meals alone did not shift it. Scheer et al. (2009) [[1]](#pm-ref-1); Leproult et al. (1997) [[14]](#pm-ref-14); Jamshed et al. (2019) [[16]](#pm-ref-16); Wehrens et al. (2017) [[7]](#pm-ref-7). Light and a prebiotic changed acute levels or the waking rise rather than a measured rhythm, and light acted in either direction depending on timing. Jung et al. (2010) [[13]](#pm-ref-13); Schmidt et al. (2015) [[3]](#pm-ref-3). Youths with ADHD have lower morning cortisol, but the size of the gap depends heavily on two studies of young children sampled around stressful events. Chang et al. (2021) [[2]](#pm-ref-2). No study tested correcting an abnormal rhythm.

## 5. BRS Pathways and Connections

### 5.1 BRS Pathways

No newly adjudicated BRS pathway is established by this assessment.

### 5.2 Cross-BRS Mechanism Relationships

- [BRS-X(Circadian-FM1) — Light and Feeding Zeitgeber Entrainment](/docs/biological-targets/brs-x/circadian/fm1/brs-x-circadian-fm1-zeitgeber-entrainment) — cortisol is a rhythmic endocrine output affected during experimental human circadian misalignment ([Scheer et al., 2009](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#scheer_2009_19255424)). This supports timing–HPA coupling, without equating a stress intervention with clock entrainment or establishing an ADHD treatment effect.
- [BRS-X Circadian PM1 — Light–Dark Circadian Entrainment](/docs/biological-targets/brs-x/circadian/fm1/brs-x-circadian-fm1-pm1-light-dark-circadian-entrainment) — light changes cortisol acutely, in a direction set by timing ([Scheer and Buijs, 1999](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#scheer_buijs_light_cortisol_1999); [Jung et al., 2010](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#jung_bright_light_cortisol_2010)); clock entrainment by light is assessed there, not here.
- [BRS-X Circadian PM2 — Feeding-Time Circadian Synchronisation](/docs/biological-targets/brs-x/circadian/fm1/brs-x-circadian-fm1-pm2-feeding-time-circadian-synchronisation) — meal delay shifted peripheral clocks but not cortisol phase ([Wehrens et al., 2017](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#wehrens_meal_timing_2017)).

No downstream mapping is inherited from a shared nutrient or KC candidate.

### 5.3 Local BRS Mechanism Relationships

[BRS6(FM3) PM6 — Sympathetic Activation and Parasympathetic Recovery](/docs/biological-targets/brs6/fm3/brs6-fm3-pm6-sympathetic-activation-and-parasympathetic-recovery) addresses autonomic recovery after challenge; the electrodermal recovery result from Lane et al. (2010) [[5]](#pm-ref-5) belongs there rather than to cortisol rhythm.
