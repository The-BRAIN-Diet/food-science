summary: 'Acetylcholine is made when choline acetyltransferase joins choline to acetyl-CoA inside cholinergic nerve terminals. Choline reaches this reaction through separate blood–brain and terminal-uptake transport systems, while acetyl-CoA comes from compartmented neuronal metabolism. Diet provides choline directly or through phosphatidylcholine, and pantothenate supports coenzyme A formation. These dietary routes contribute to substrate provision, while terminal transport and cellular metabolism still govern access to the reaction.'

  - id: PM6-F6
    finding_label: One-carbon insufficiency can constrain the choline resource used for acetylcholine
    finding_summary: Folate- and B12-dependent one-carbon metabolism shares choline-related methyl resources. Dietary or genetic impairment can lower brain choline and acetylcholine in animal models.
    finding_interpretation: 'This supports a constrained-by mapping to the Methyl Donor Pool. It does not make folate, B12, or betaine ChAT cofactors or separate Direct Dietary Requirements.'
    finding_statement: 'Inadequate folate-dependent or B12-dependent methionine-cycle capacity can disturb brain choline metabolism and lower acetylcholine availability, creating a supported shared-resource constraint on acetylcholine-synthesis capacity.'
    synthesised_evidence_confidence: not-yet-scored
    synthesis: 'Dietary folate compromise lowered brain choline and acetylcholine in mice, with SAM restoring both under the deficient condition. Genetic methionine-synthase-reductase impairment likewise lowered hippocampal choline, betaine, and acetylcholine. A separate rat study found mixed, region- and age-dependent acetylcholine changes, showing that the effect is not uniform. Together these sources establish a shared-resource constraint while preserving biological variability.'
    synthesis_limitations: 'Animal dietary and genetic models measure metabolite pools more often than synthesis flux. They do not show that folate, B12, betaine, or SAM supplementation raises acetylcholine synthesis in nutrient-replete humans.'

## BRS1-FM2-PM6 - Acetylcholine Synthesis Support

(Supplying the Two Substrates Used to Make Acetylcholine)

## 1. Mission & Overview + Dietary Levers

### Mission

Maintain the capacity to form acetylcholine so cholinergic signalling for attention, working memory, and learning stays supplied.


Diet directly supplies choline and supplies phosphatidylcholine as a choline-provision route. Pantothenate supports the separate acetyl-CoA substrate route through coenzyme A. These dependencies support a Diet-Dominant profile, but they do not establish that extra intake raises acetylcholine in the human brain.

**Intervention Dominance:** Diet-Dominant

### Overview

Acetylcholine is made when choline acetyltransferase joins choline to acetyl-CoA inside cholinergic nerve terminals (nerve endings that use acetylcholine). Oda (1999) [[4]](#pm-ref-4) establishes this reaction. Choline reaches it through separate blood–brain and terminal-uptake transport systems [Inazu (2019) [[13]](#pm-ref-13); Ojiakor and Rylett (2020) [[6]](#pm-ref-6)], while acetyl-CoA comes from compartmented neuronal metabolism [Jope (1979) [[12]](#pm-ref-12)]. Diet provides choline directly or through phosphatidylcholine, and pantothenate supports coenzyme A formation [Derbyshire and Maes (2023) [[1]](#pm-ref-1); Böckmann et al. (2023) [[7]](#pm-ref-7); Rivera-Calimlim et al. (1988) [[8]](#pm-ref-8)]. These dietary routes contribute to substrate provision, while terminal transport and cellular metabolism still govern access to the reaction.

* **Mechanism boundary:** Covers acetylcholine formation, not its vesicular storage, release, receptor signalling or breakdown [Oda (1999) [[4]](#pm-ref-4); Ojiakor and Rylett (2020) [[6]](#pm-ref-6)].
* **Evidence boundary:** Tissue acetylcholine concentration and acetylcholine release do not by themselves measure synthesis rate [Crivello et al. (2010) [[10]](#pm-ref-10); Szutowicz et al. (2010) [[14]](#pm-ref-14)].
* **Biological relevance:** Cholinergic biology is relevant to attention and learning, but the assessed evidence does not establish a nutritional treatment effect [Johansson et al. (2013) [[3]](#pm-ref-3); Derbyshire and Maes (2023) [[1]](#pm-ref-1)].

## 4. Mechanistic Basis

### Summary

Cholinergic signalling depends on a nerve terminal having both reaction substrates at the right place and time. Choline must first enter through a high-affinity transporter; choline acetyltransferase then transfers an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA. This synthesis capacity is different from the amount of acetylcholine stored, released, or detected at receptors.

#### (Choline entry is a regulated supply step)

Choline reaching the brain and choline entering a cholinergic nerve terminal are separate steps. CTL1/CTL2 transport systems operate at brain microvascular endothelium, whereas the high-affinity transporter CHT/SLC5A7 recovers choline into cholinergic nerve terminals. CHT activity and movement to the cell surface respond to neuronal activity, making terminal transport a major control point rather than a passive reflection of how much choline was eaten. Ojiakor and Rylett (2020) [[6]](#pm-ref-6) review terminal transport; Inazu (2019) [[13]](#pm-ref-13) reviews the blood–brain boundary.

#### (Two substrates form acetylcholine)

Inside the terminal, choline acetyltransferase transfers the acetyl group from acetyl-CoA to choline, forming acetylcholine and coenzyme A. Choline and acetyl-CoA are therefore reaction substrates; the enzyme does not use folate, vitamin B12, or pantothenate directly as cofactors. Oda (1999) [[4]](#pm-ref-4) establishes the enzyme chemistry.

#### (Acetyl-CoA has its own compartmented supply route)

ChAT uses acetyl-CoA in the cytosol, but much neuronal acetyl-CoA is generated in mitochondria. Acetyl units can reach the cytosolic pool through routes including citrate export and ATP-citrate-lyase cleavage, alongside other proposed pyruvate-dependent or mitochondrial-export routes. Their relative contribution is not fully settled, but acetyl-CoA availability can regulate synthesis independently of choline transport. Jope (1979) [[12]](#pm-ref-12) reviews this evidence. It does not make glucose, pyruvate, citrate, or dietary acetyl-CoA separate requirements on this page.

#### (Dietary provision reaches the two substrates by different routes)

Phosphatidylcholine is a choline-containing dietary form. Human tracer evidence shows that it is digested and remodelled, with labelled choline appearing in plasma; it is therefore a provision route to choline, not another ChAT substrate. Böckmann et al. (2023) [[7]](#pm-ref-7) measured this route. Pantothenate supports the other side of the reaction by supplying coenzyme A for acetyl-CoA formation. Severe pantothenate depletion reduced labelled brain acetylcholine synthesis in a chronic rat model, and pantothenate co-administration increased it. Rivera-Calimlim et al. (1988) [[8]](#pm-ref-8) do not establish a routine human supplementation effect.

#### (A shared one-carbon resource can change choline availability)

Choline can also be oxidised to betaine for one-carbon metabolism (the network that transfers methyl groups). Folate compromise and genetic impairment of B12-dependent methionine-cycle function lowered brain choline and acetylcholine in animal models, although regional responses were not uniform. Chan et al. (2008) [[9]](#pm-ref-9), Crivello et al. (2010) [[10]](#pm-ref-10), and Jadavji et al. (2014) [[11]](#pm-ref-11) support a shared-resource constraint, not a claim that folate, B12, or betaine run the ChAT reaction.

#### (Boundaries of the mechanism)

Synthesis ends when acetylcholine has been formed. Packaging by the vesicular acetylcholine transporter, release into the synapse, receptor binding, breakdown by acetylcholinesterase, and the resulting cognitive effects are later stages. Acetylcholine concentration also reflects storage, release, and breakdown, so concentration alone is not a synthesis-rate measurement.

#### (Integration within BRS1)

This synthesis capacity supports [BRS1(FM2) — Cholinergic Function](/docs/biological-targets/brs1/fm2/brs1-fm2-cholinergic-function). Choline allocation also intersects BRS2 remethylation and phosphatidylcholine biology, while the [BRS2(KC1) — Methyl Donor Pool](/docs/biological-targets/brs2/kc/brs2-kc1-one-carbon-donor-pool) can constrain how much choline remains available for the synthesis substrate pool.

### 5.2 Cross-BRS Mechanism Relationships

Primary Mechanisms in other Biological Regulatory Systems that directly interact with, constrain or support this mechanism.

- [BRS2-FM1-PM1 — Folate/B12-Dependent Homocysteine Remethylation](/docs/biological-targets/brs2/fm1/brs2-fm1-pm1-folate-b12-dependent-homocysteine-remethylation) — Folate- and B12-dependent remethylation provides an alternative to choline-derived betaine for methyl donation. When this route is impaired, animal evidence shows lower brain choline and acetylcholine, creating a downstream substrate constraint here.
- [BRS2-FM1-PM2 — Betaine/BHMT Remethylation](/docs/biological-targets/brs2/fm1/brs2-fm1-pm2-betaine-bhmt-remethylation) — Oxidation of choline supplies betaine to this remethylation route. That competing use links methyl-donor demand to choline availability, but dietary betaine has not been shown here to increase human brain acetylcholine synthesis.
- [BRS2-FM3-PM7 — Phosphatidylcholine Formation](/docs/biological-targets/brs2/fm3/brs2-fm3-pm7-phosphatidylcholine-formation) — Phosphatidylcholine is a major choline-containing membrane and dietary form. Its digestion can provide circulating choline, while its synthesis and membrane roles remain distinct from the ChAT reaction.
