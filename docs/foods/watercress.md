## Overview

Watercress is a peppery brassica salad leaf providing **vitamin C**, **vitamin K**, and carotenoids including **lutein**, **zeaxanthin** and **beta-carotene**. It also forms **isothiocyanates** from glucosinolates when the leaf is chewed or otherwise disrupted [1].

Within a BRAIN-aligned pattern it adds crucifer diversity to mixed salads rather than acting as a concentrated isothiocyanate supplement. Eating it raw preserves plant myrosinase; boiling reduces conversion [1]. Dietary fat in the same meal supports carotenoid absorption [2].

## Food Context

### Synergies

- Pair with a fat-containing dressing so carotenoids from the leaf are absorbed more effectively [2].
- Include among other brassicas when the aim is glucosinolate diversity rather than a single crucifer.

### Preparation

- Use raw in salads, removing only coarse stems. Uncooked watercress yields substantially more isothiocyanate uptake than watercress boiled long enough to deactivate myrosinase [1].
- Do not treat watercress as interchangeable with lettuce; it is a brassica, not a chicory or cos leaf.

## References

[1] Getahun & Chung (1999). [Conversion of glucosinolates to isothiocyanates in humans after ingestion of cooked watercress](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#getahun_watercress_1999). After cooked watercress with deactivated myrosinase, urinary isothiocyanate conjugates accounted for 1.2–7.3% of ingested glucosinolates; after uncooked watercress, excretion ranged from 17.2–77.7% of ingested isothiocyanates.

[2] Unlu et al. (2005). [Carotenoid Absorption from Salad and Salsa by Humans Is Enhanced by the Addition of Avocado or Avocado Oil](/docs/papers/BRAIN-Diet-References#unlu_carotenoid_2005). Adding avocado fruit or avocado oil to salad increased human absorption of α-carotene, β-carotene and lutein versus the same salad without added lipid.
