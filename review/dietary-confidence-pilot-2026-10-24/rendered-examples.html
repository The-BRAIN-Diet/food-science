BRS1-FM4-PM9: Vitamin B6

Dietary Confidence: High — Dietary B6 supplies precursors of the PLP cofactor required by GAD. This is a capacity relationship, not evidence that extra B6 increases brain GABA.

Relevant Findings: BRS1-FM4-PM9 / PM9-F1; BRS1-FM4-PM9 / PM9-F2

Evidence & Audit

Assessment ID: DC-BRS1-FM4-PM9-PM9-DIT-3

Assessed proposition: Dietary vitamin B6 supplies precursors that sustain PLP-dependent glutamate-decarboxylase capacity for GABA synthesis.

Dietary Confidence: High

Relationship: Type A — Capacity; derived

Evidence rationale: High is justified for the defined capacity proposition by convergent GAD enzymology, human B6 activation enzymology and dietary utilisation evidence. Martin and Rimvall is secondary synthesis, not an independent experiment. Human PDXK and PNPO experiments distinguish phosphorylation from oxidation; primary nutritional-deficiency evidence supports cofactor-dependent brain GAD capacity. Missing trials of extra intake, neuronal PLP occupancy or ADHD outcomes do not weaken this narrower established dependency. Plant glucoside bioavailability and pathological activation defects limit supply in specified contexts rather than negate the relationship.

Evidence coherence: Not separately graded; see the recorded evidence rationale above.

Engagement, dietary delivery and supporting sources

PM-specific capacity (Type A): PLP-dependent GAD catalysis is established; dietary deficiency can reduce rat-brain GAD activity through cofactor depletion. The proposition is capacity, not increased synthesis at sufficient intake.

Recorded support: supported

  • https://www.nature.com/articles/nsmb1228
  • https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1471-4159.1972.tb01374.x

Dietary delivery: Human tracer studies establish utilisation of oral B6, including incomplete availability of PN-glucoside. PDXK and human PNPO experiments support vitamer-specific conversion to PLP; this does not measure neuronal cofactor occupancy.

Recorded support: supported

  • https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1995788/
  • https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17766369/
  • https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2323923/

  • BRS1-FM4-PM9 / PM9-F2 — Existing mechanistic Finding; confidence does not inherit its SEC score.

Scope and confidence limitations

Assessed scope: Biochemical capacity under functioning B6 absorption, activation and cofactor handling; not quantitative neuronal occupancy, increased GABA from extra intake, or clinical efficacy.

  • Pyridoxal is phosphorylated to PLP; pyridoxine and pyridoxamine are phosphorylated to PNP/PMP and require PNPO-dependent oxidation. This clarification does not edit the preserved historical atom.
  • Food matrices and glycosylated vitamers differ in availability; normal activation cannot be assumed in PNPO/PDXK disorders.
  • No predictable brain GABA response, dose recommendation, benefit above adequacy or ADHD efficacy is established; PLP is not GABA-specific.

Hypothesis-1 — Resistant Starch–Derived Butyrate and Tight-Junction Regulation Unestablished

Scientific hypothesis. Scientific admission is required before any proposed mechanism relationship can be approved, including when a hypothesis is marked Supported.

Proposition: Dietary resistant starch may regulate epithelial paracellular tight-junction function through microbial butyrate production and local exposure.

Proposed direction: BRS5-FM2-PM5 → BRS5-FM1-PM1

Rationale: Selected resistant-starch preparations support microbial butyrate production, and direct butyrate exposure can influence epithelial junction assembly. These separate observations motivate testing the joined dietary pathway; they do not establish it. The proposed five-link chain is: (1) dietary resistant-starch substrate reaches a capable microbial community; (2) utilisation and cross-feeding generate a context-dependent butyrate pool; (3) luminal butyrate becomes available to and is taken up by the epithelium; (4) cellular butyrate exposure engages pathways such as AMPK-related tight-junction assembly; (5) local engagement would produce a demonstrable paracellular tight-junction functional effect in the relevant host context. Evidence for separate links does not establish the complete chain. Moreau’s increased uptake followed healing; Peng’s cellular mechanism and the bounded human null/transcellular results must not be joined into an established dietary effect.

Supporting Findings

None identified as directly testing this proposition.

Contradictory or null Findings

None identified as directly testing this proposition.

Contextual Findings
  • BRS5-FM2-PM5 / PM5-F1 — Supports community- and formulation-dependent upstream fermentation, not a downstream tight-junction mechanism.
  • BRS5-FM1-PM1 / PM1-F2 — Butyrate-exposed epithelial models inform plausibility, not dietary resistant-starch mediation.
Source evidence and its role
  • Peng et al. (2009) (contextual) — Direct butyrate exposure supports AMPK-related junction assembly in Caco-2 monolayers. Purified exposure and cell model; not resistant-starch-derived epithelial exposure.
  • Moreau et al. (2004) (contextual) — In RS3-fed colitic rats, increased mucosal butyrate utilisation followed healing. Infused labelled butyrate uptake was not tracing starch carbon. Histological healing and substrate use do not establish paracellular tight-junction mediation.
  • Acute human colonic biopsy experiment (2020) (contradictory_or_null) — Acute 5 or 25 mM butyrate pretreatment did not prevent induced hyperpermeability ex vivo. Chemical injury and acute exposure; not a universal refutation of dietary or chronic effects.
  • Controlled potato/bagel dietary trial (2022) (contradictory_or_null) — The tested potato diet lowered small-intestinal permeability indices but not the colonic probe; it did not establish butyrate-mediated tight-junction regulation. Metabolic syndrome, short dietary periods and surrogate permeability measures; formulation effects and alternative pathways remain.
  • Scharf et al. (2025) (contextual) — Direct 100 mmol/L colonic butyrate perfusion for 90 minutes reduced induced transcellular hyperpermeability in IBS biopsies compared with pre-exposure. Transcellular outcome is not demonstrated paracellular tight-junction regulation. Direct perfusion is not dietary delivery and does not establish the resistant-starch pathway.

References to the same study or cohort are not independent evidence. Finding scores are not inherited.

Principal limitations
  • No joined dietary resistant-starch → butyrate → paracellular tight-junction mechanism has been established by the evidence reviewed here. This is a bounded assessment, not a claim that no such study exists anywhere.
  • Starch preparation, microbial degraders/cross-feeding, regional exposure and host state affect the pathway.
  • Faecal butyrate is not production flux or epithelial exposure; transcripts, electrical resistance, histology and paracellular marker flux are distinct endpoints.
  • Do not collapse human transcellular protection or small-intestinal permeability improvement into a colonic paracellular tight-junction result.
Alternative or interacting explanations
  • Other fermentation products, microbial shifts, altered bile acids, immune effects or nonfermentation food components may mediate a barrier result.
  • Mucosal healing may increase butyrate uptake rather than be caused by it.
Evidence required
  • A defined dietary preparation linked to local butyrate exposure and appropriate paracellular/junction endpoints, with credible causal mediation and microbial/host context addressed.
  • Starch-carbon tracing is one useful approach, not a mandatory exclusive admission criterion; perturbation, rescue and convergent mediation evidence may also be informative.
  • Separate paracellular from transcellular effects and measured exposure from inferred metabolite availability.
