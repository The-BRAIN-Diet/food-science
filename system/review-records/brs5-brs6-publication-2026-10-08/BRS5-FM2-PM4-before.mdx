summary: >-
  Gut microbes compete for available substrates and adjust the machinery used to
  process them. Experiments manipulating community membership and fibre
  structure show that resource access, protein expression and glycan degradation
  depend on community context Patnode et al. (2019). Human dietary evidence also
  shows that processing potential can change without increased diversity Wastyk
  et al. (2021). This PM concerns how available substrates shape microbial
  competition and processing functions. Community turnover may accompany these
  changes, but is not itself a measure of improved function.

## BRS5-FM2-PM4 - Microbial Substrate-Processing Selection & Adaptation

(Community Competition and Adaptation in Substrate Use)

## 1. Mission & Overview

### Mission


Adjust community substrate-processing capacity through resource competition and changes in microbial function.


**Intervention Dominance:** Diet-Supported

### Overview

Gut microbes compete for available substrates and adjust the machinery used to process them. Experiments manipulating community membership and fibre structure show that resource access, protein expression and glycan degradation depend on community context Patnode et al. (2019) [[5]](#pm-ref-5). Human dietary evidence also shows that processing potential can change without increased diversity Wastyk et al. (2021) [[1]](#pm-ref-1). This PM concerns how available substrates shape microbial competition and processing functions. Community turnover may accompany these changes, but is not itself a measure of improved function.

- **Mechanism boundary:** Community resource-use competition and functional acclimation belong here; specific SCFA production/signalling (PM5), polyphenol conversion (PM6) and precursor handling (PM8) retain their own jobs Patnode et al. (2019) [[5]](#pm-ref-5).
- **Measurement boundary:** Gene potential, protein expression and measured substrate degradation establish different links. More diversity or a longer time course does not demonstrate greater activity Wastyk et al. (2021) [[1]](#pm-ref-1); Patnode et al. (2019) [[5]](#pm-ref-5).
- **Biological relevance:** Resource-use changes can affect host conditions, including adverse mucus effects in models. Human immune responses must be preserved without assuming ecological changes caused them Wastyk et al. (2021) [[1]](#pm-ref-1); Desai et al. (2016) [[6]](#pm-ref-6).

## 2. Primary Biological Effects

Substrate-dependent microbial competition; changes in utilisation machinery and community processing capacity. The direction of a host outcome must be assessed separately.

## 4. Mechanistic Basis

### Summary

Available substrates and competing community members influence which utilisation functions are expressed and which resources are processed Patnode et al. (2019) [[5]](#pm-ref-5). This includes acclimation within an existing community, not only replacement of taxa.

### Competition and processing machinery

Defined-community experiments distinguish utilisation-gene possession from protein expression, resource-use fitness and actual glycan degradation Patnode et al. (2019) [[5]](#pm-ref-5). Diet-associated CAZyme gene enrichment in humans supplies a separate potential-level measurement; it does not measure enzyme flux Wastyk et al. (2021) [[1]](#pm-ref-1).

### Host consequences and boundaries

Resource-use shifts can be adverse: a fibre-deprived defined community increased host-mucin use alongside mucus injury Desai et al. (2016) [[6]](#pm-ref-6). Increased diversity and reduced inflammatory proteins accompanied fermented-food intake in a human trial, without establishing diversity-mediated benefit Wastyk et al. (2021) [[1]](#pm-ref-1). PM4 owns the community resource-use process; PM5, PM6 and PM8 own their specific products and conversions.

### 4.1 Scientific Findings

#### Summary

Community competition and substrate-use adaptation have experimental activity-level evidence. Human gene-potential and immune findings remain distinct measurements; neither diversity nor turnover is a universal benefit target.

Primary Mechanisms in other Biological Regulatory Systems that directly interact with, constrain or support this mechanism.
